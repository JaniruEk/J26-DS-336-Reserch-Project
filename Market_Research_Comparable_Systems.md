# Market & Literature Research: Comparable Systems to J26-DS-336

## Distributed Multi-Agent SLM Framework for Privacy-Preserving Enterprise Data Intelligence

**Purpose.** This is a critical landscape check, not a literature-review draft for the proposal. It asks two questions honestly: (1) does something like this already exist, and (2) given what exists, where does the current project design need to improve to stay defensible as a final-year research contribution. Findings below are organized around each of the four components plus the project's overall framing, with the sharpest comparisons first.

**Update note:** the team has since dropped the independent OLAP/MDX research pillar and reverted to a four-agent sequential pipeline — Ekanayake (retrieval), Wathsala (foundation model), Thathsarani (fine-tuning-architecture selection: standard LoRA vs. HydraLoRA), Karunanayake (quantization-strategy selection: QAT vs. PTQ, plus a cross-check against Thathsarani's runner-up architecture). This document has been updated to match: the OLAP/MDX section below has been removed, and the fine-tuning/quantization findings are now attributed to Thathsarani and Karunanayake respectively rather than to a single merged "Karunanayake" component.

## 1. The Closest Direct Competitor: MATS (Multi-Agent Text2SQL with SLMs)

**arXiv 2512.18622**, "A Multi-agent Text2SQL Framework using Small Language Models and Execution Feedback" — this is the single most structurally similar system found, and the team should read it closely before finalizing the Ekanayake/Karunanayake pipeline narrative.

MATS is a five-agent pipeline, all built on small language models deployable on a single consumer GPU:
- Schema Insight Agent — filters irrelevant schema, retrieves matching column values (RoBERTa-large)
- Planner Agent — decomposes reasoning, generates multiple SQL candidates
- Validator Agent — checks candidates against database execution, with Selection and Condition sub-validators
- Fix Agent — refines SQL based on validator feedback
- Selection Agent — picks the best candidate

Model budget: 9B parameters total across the five agents (LLaMA-3.2 3B for Planner/Selection, LLaMA-3.2 1B for Validator/Fix), running on a single 24GB GPU. It's trained with "Reinforcement Learning with Execution Feedback" (RLEF) — an ORPO-based method that uses database execution results as an automatic reward signal instead of human labels. Results: 87.1% execution accuracy on Spider, 64.73% on BIRD — competitive with GPT-4-class systems at a fraction of the parameter count. Acknowledged weaknesses: subqueries still drop accuracy 45-72%, synonym-heavy questions are harder, and inference is slow (14-22 seconds per query across five agent hops).

**What this means for the project.** The headline pitch — "small, locally-hostable agents instead of one large model, coordinating on schema retrieval → generation → validation/repair" — is not a novel systems-level idea anymore; MATS (and the broader multi-agent Text2SQL literature below) already established that shape and published strong numbers on it in 2025-2026. This is not fatal, but it changes what the project can credibly claim as its contribution. It should not claim novelty for "multi-agent small-model text-to-SQL" as a category. What it can still credibly claim, and should lean on harder in the write-up:

- MATS uses five separately fine-tuned models totaling 9B parameters and needs a 24GB GPU. J26-DS-336's target is a single shared base model with swappable LoRA/HydraLoRA adapters plus quantization, aimed at mid-range/8GB-class hardware — a meaningfully smaller footprint if the numbers hold up. This efficiency delta is measurable and should be reported explicitly against MATS's published figures as a comparison point, not just asserted.
- MATS does not do complexity-specialized routing (HydraLoRA-style multi-head adaptation keyed to query complexity) — its Planner/Fix split is by pipeline stage, not by query difficulty class. The HydraLoRA-for-complexity-routing idea (Thathsarani's fine-tuning-architecture component) is not present in MATS or, as far as this search found, anywhere else in the text-to-SQL literature (see Section 4).
- MATS does not study quantization strategy at all — it assumes fp16/bf16 small models fit on a 24GB card. The QAT-vs-PTQ comparison plus the cross-check against Thathsarani's runner-up architecture (Karunanayake's component) is genuinely additive relative to this closest comparator.

**Recommendation:** cite MATS explicitly in the literature review as the nearest prior system, and frame the project's contribution as depth on two axes MATS doesn't touch (complexity-aware fine-tuning architecture selection, and quantization-strategy selection) plus a cross-language transferability study, rather than as "the first multi-agent SLM text-to-SQL system" — that claim will not survive a defense panel that has seen this paper.

Related multi-agent text-to-SQL work worth at least a citation, confirming this is a busy sub-field: MAC-SQL (ACL 2025, the earlier and more-cited multi-agent decomposition framework this area builds on), CHASE-SQL (ICLR 2025, multi-path reasoning + preference-based candidate selection), and MARS-SQL (multi-agent reinforcement learning framing, arXiv 2511.01008). None of these specifically target small/local models the way MATS and this project do — most assume large proprietary-API models — so the "small-model, locally-hostable" framing is still a meaningful differentiator against MAC-SQL/CHASE-SQL specifically, even though it's shared with MATS.

## 2. Privacy-Preserving Framing: Real, But Contested Ground

Two existing lines of work attack "privacy-preserving text-to-SQL" from different angles than this project, and the proposal should be precise about which kind of privacy claim it's making:

- **MaskSQL** ("Safeguarding Privacy for LLM-Based Text-to-SQL via Abstraction") takes the opposite architectural approach: instead of hosting a small model locally, it lets you keep using a large proprietary-API model (like GPT-4) but strips/abstracts sensitive schema and data details before they leave the enterprise, then reconstructs the real query afterward. This is a "privacy via redaction" approach, not a "privacy via local hosting" approach.
- **Prem-1B-SQL** (Prem AI) is the closest commercial/open artifact to what Wathsala's component is trying to produce: a 1.3B-parameter model fully fine-tuned from DeepSeek-1.3B specifically for text-to-SQL, explicitly marketed as "local-first" so "data is not exposed to third-party APIs." It's trained on BirdBench/Spider plus synthetic data (GretelAI) and a custom error-handling set, using execution-guided decoding with up to 5 retry attempts on failure — notably similar in spirit to Karunanayake's execution/error-diagnosis retry sub-agent. Reported results: 51.54% EX on BIRD's private test set (beating Claude 2 and Qwen2.5-Coder-7B at the time), 85% on Spider validation. Critically: **it does no schema retrieval/pruning and no multi-agent orchestration** — schema is simply appended whole to the prompt.

**What this means:** the project's "privacy-preserving" claim (data never leaves the organization because everything runs on small local models) is a real and increasingly common positioning — not unique, but legitimate and worth keeping. The more specific claim worth emphasizing is the combination Prem-1B-SQL and MaskSQL each only do half of: local-only hosting (like Prem-1B-SQL) combined with active schema retrieval/pruning before generation (which Prem-1B-SQL skips entirely). That combination — plus the complexity-routed fine-tuning and quantization-strategy research on top — is where the actual gap sits, not in "privacy" as a headline claim by itself.

**Precision check — don't overstate what's legally "prohibited."** An early framing risk worth flagging explicitly: it's tempting to write the problem statement as "regulation legally prohibits sending data to third parties," but that's not accurate and is exactly the kind of claim a defense panel will poke a hole in — regulated enterprises (banks especially) share data with third parties constantly: payment processors, core-banking software vendors, cloud hosting, KYC/verification providers, all under audited contracts and data-processing agreements built up over years. The precise claim is narrower: sending live schema and customer data into a *generative-AI vendor specifically* is restricted at most such organisations — not purely by law, but by internal AI-governance and vendor-risk policy that treats generative-AI vendors as a newer, less-precedented category (open questions around prompt logging, retention, training-data use, and auditability) than an established third-party relationship. GDPR/CCPA reinforce this caution but don't single-handedly prohibit it — an enterprise-tier AI vendor contract with a proper data-processing agreement could in principle satisfy the letter of the law, yet most regulated enterprises' internal policy still won't clear it, because the vendor-risk approval process for a broad, free-form "send arbitrary schema and data, get arbitrary text back" data flow hasn't caught up to how established a payment-processor relationship is. Recommend rewriting the proposal's problem statement (Section 1.1 Background, all individual proposals, and the project scope doc) to use this narrower, more defensible framing rather than the blanket "cloud AI = illegal" version — this has already been done in project-scope, the combined proposal, and Ekanayake's individual proposal as of this update.

## 3. Schema Retrieval & Pruning (Ekanayake): A Crowded, Well-Studied Sub-Problem

Schema linking/pruning for text-to-SQL is one of the most active sub-areas in this literature — GRASP (graph-enhanced retrieval for schema pruning), RASL (Amazon Science, retrieval-augmented schema linking for massive databases), RSL-SQL (robust schema linking), SchemaGraphSQL, and AST-based retrieval-augmented pruning (EMNLP 2024) all address close variants of exactly what Agent 1 does: reduce a large schema to the relevant subset before generation.

**What needs improvement here:** this is the component most likely to draw a "hasn't this been done" question in a defense, because it has the most direct prior art. The mitigating factor is that Ekanayake's design already includes something several of these don't foreground as cleanly — a documented four-mode ablation (semantic-anchor / keyword-anchor / full-traversal / no-pruning-baseline) that quantifies the pruning step's own contribution rather than treating retrieval as a fixed preprocessing black box. The proposal should explicitly position this component's contribution as the ablation/measurement rigor and its integration into a complexity-aware downstream pipeline, not as a novel retrieval algorithm — GRASP and RASL are already more sophisticated on pure retrieval technique (graph neural approaches, learned rerankers) than a ChromaDB-plus-spaCy pipeline is likely to be. Read GRASP and RASL specifically before finalizing Ekanayake's methodology section; if either is cited as related work without acknowledging they already solve a harder version of the same retrieval problem, that's a gap a reviewer will find.

## 4. Fine-Tuning Architecture (Thathsarani) + Quantization (Karunanayake): The Strongest Novelty Claim, With One Risk

**HydraLoRA** itself (NeurIPS 2024 Oral, and its close cousins Hydra/R-LoRA) is a general-purpose asymmetric multi-head LoRA architecture — shared down-projection, multiple task-specialized up-projection heads — but it was proposed and evaluated as a general multi-task PEFT method, not applied to text-to-SQL complexity routing specifically. This search found no existing paper that applies HydraLoRA-style routing keyed to *query complexity class* within text-to-SQL. That is a genuinely available novelty gap, and it's the strongest original-contribution claim across all four components as currently scoped.

The risk: because HydraLoRA's routing is normally *learned* (a gating mechanism decides which head to use), while this project's design uses a *hard-coded* rule-based router (the shared complexity classifier), a reviewer familiar with the HydraLoRA paper may ask why the routing isn't learned end-to-end. Have a ready answer: a deterministic router is more auditable/debuggable (you always know which head produced which SQL) and removes a training-stability risk (a bad gating network can route everything to one head early in training and never recover) — but this is worth stating as a deliberate design tradeoff in the proposal, not leaving it for the defense to surface first.

On quantization: general QAT-vs-PTQ comparison guides and tooling writeups (GGUF/AWQ/GPTQ explainers) are abundant, but this search found no controlled academic study comparing QAT vs. PTQ specifically for text-to-SQL-fine-tuned small models, and nothing resembling the Cross-Check design (re-testing the winning quantization strategy against the runner-up fine-tuning architecture to check ranking stability). That combination remains a legitimate, fairly narrow but real gap. One adjacent paper worth pulling into the literature review regardless — "How Small Can You Go? A Controlled Study of LoRA Rank, Target Modules, and Quantization Trade-offs for Text-to-SQL on a 60M-Parameter Model" (arXiv 2607.25583) — is close enough in spirit (LoRA + quantization trade-offs, text-to-SQL, small model) that it should be read and either cited or explicitly distinguished from (it studies LoRA rank/target-module trade-offs on a single tiny 60M model; it does not compare LoRA vs. HydraLoRA as competing architectures, nor QAT vs. PTQ as competing quantization strategies, nor run a cross-check — but it is close enough that an examiner may already know it and ask how this project differs).

## 5. Commercial Landscape: Different Market, Worth One Paragraph, Not More

Snowflake Cortex Analyst, Databricks Genie, ThoughtSpot Spotter/Sage, and a growing list of 2026-era entrants (Promethium, Querio, and others surfaced in comparison-blog results) are all cloud-hosted, large-proprietary-model-backed natural-language analytics tools sold to enterprises already on those platforms. They are not privacy-preserving in the sense this project means it (queries and schema typically do go to a hosted LLM service, even if wrapped in the vendor's own compliance controls), they are not open about model architecture or fine-tuning method, and none position themselves as small-model/on-prem by design — some vendors' pitch is closer to "trust our governance" than "the model literally never leaves your infrastructure." This makes them a useful one-paragraph contrast in the introduction (this is the market backdrop: enterprises adopting NL-to-SQL tools that require sending schema/data to a hosted third-party model) but not a direct technical comparator — they're a different category (product) from this project and MATS/Prem-1B-SQL/etc. (research/open architecture), and the proposal shouldn't spend much space benchmarking against them since architectural details aren't published.

These same tools are also the proof that "ad hoc natural-language querying beyond what a dashboard covers" is real, proven demand, not a speculative pitch — Snowflake and Databricks are selling exactly that capability at scale. The gap this project's target market (regulated enterprises that can't use a cloud-hosted vendor) faces isn't that ad hoc NL querying doesn't exist as a product category; it's that the products delivering it aren't usable under those enterprises' AI-governance restrictions (Section 2). Worth stating this explicitly in the introduction so the "why not just use a dashboard" and "why not just use Cortex Analyst" objections are both preempted in the same paragraph.

## 6. Summary Table: What's Genuinely Defensible vs. What Needs Reframing

| Component | Closest existing work | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Overall multi-agent SLM pipeline shape | MATS (arXiv 2512.18622) | Not novel as a category anymore — reframe around efficiency (single shared model + adapters vs. MATS's 9B/5-model setup) and depth on fine-tuning/quantization research, not pipeline shape |
| "Privacy-preserving" framing | MaskSQL (redaction-based), Prem-1B-SQL (local-hosting-based) | Legitimate, but be precise: claim is local-hosting + active schema pruning combined, not an unclaimed category — and the restriction itself is AI-governance/vendor-risk policy on generative-AI vendors specifically, not a blanket "no third parties" claim |
